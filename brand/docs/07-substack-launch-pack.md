## 1. What changed and why

| Before (public Substack) | Now (The Margin Wars Briefing) |
|---|---|
| Public, weekly, free; growth to 4,000+ subscribers (Base) | Invite-only and approved one reader at a time; **monthly**; capped at about **150 named operators** (10_Standout_Strategy rung 0) |
| Thematic essays ("stablecoins are a ledger problem", "where the money sits") | **Tactical playbooks: one specific move per issue**, written in a fixed tactic-card format |
| Sponsorship once the list passed 2,500 | **No sponsorship and no ads.** Vendor money goes to the Working Sessions instead (see 03, line R15) |
| Press passes would come from audience size | A private list gives little press value (Section 8). Speaker passes are the realistic route |
| Subscriber count as the KPI | Subscriber count is **not** a KPI (kill criterion 4). What counts: who reads it, who replies, who comes to sessions, and who buys a teardown |

**Why:** a public payments Substack is crowded: Fintech Business Weekly, Net Interest, Brainfood, This Week in Fintech, Payments Dive and others (10_Standout_Strategy, section 1). They sell news or theses. The gap is a named operator who publishes tactic-level, margin-denominated guidance with a cross-market column, then works each tactic live with a small peer group. The difference is **access** (invite-only, small, senior) and **format** (one move per issue, compared across markets), not just content.

## 4. Format: one tactic per issue

Every issue is one specific move, written as a card for the Playbook. Length 1,200-1,800 words, plus a one-page card PDF. Sent on the first Tuesday of the month, 6:00 am PT.

| Section | What it must contain |
|---|---|
| **The move** | One sentence naming a specific action (for example, "re-route BIN range X to a second acquirer") |
| **Mechanics** | How it works, step by step, at the level a payments engineer and a finance lead can both follow |
| **Prerequisites** | Contracts, data, systems, people and approvals needed before starting |
| **Metrics and expected impact** | The metric that proves it worked, how to measure it (holdout or before/after), and an impact range. **Every number is either cited to a public source or labelled [Assumption].** No employer or client data |
| **Failure modes** | How the move goes wrong, the early warning sign, and the rollback |
| **Owner** | Who decides, who builds, who watches the number |
| **Cross-market column** | How the move differs in the US, UK/EU, Africa and APAC (rules, pricing structures, local rails). Required in every issue (kill criterion 2) |
| **War story** | [WAR STORY: CONFIRM WITH AVI], sanitised so no employer, client or customer can be identified |
| **What I'd do Monday** | Three to five first steps |

**Never in the Briefing (10_Standout_Strategy kill criteria):** news commentary, trend or "future of" pieces, macro takes, stablecoin theses (stablecoins appear only as a cost line), US-only content, unsourced impact numbers, and anything that uses employer data.

## 10. Issue 1 (complete draft)

### Re-route an under-performing BIN range to a second acquirer

*The Margin Wars Briefing - Issue 1 - Tactic card 01*

**The move.** Take a group of card BINs that approve poorly or cost too much with your main acquirer, and send that slice of traffic to a second acquirer, with a holdout so you can prove the difference.

**Why it is a margin lever.** Approval rates and costs are not uniform across issuers. Acquirers differ in how their traffic is treated by particular issuers, in their local presence, and in their pricing. A narrow, measured re-route can win back approvals and basis points without renegotiating the whole contract. [WAR STORY: CONFIRM WITH AVI - a sanitised example of a BIN-level approval or cost gap and what re-routing did or did not fix]

**Mechanics**

1. Pull 90 days of authorisation data by BIN (first 6-8 digits), issuer country, card type (debit, credit, commercial, prepaid), channel (card-on-file, customer-initiated, recurring) and decline code.
2. Rank BIN groups by lost approvals (volume x approval gap against your portfolio average) and by cost per approved transaction.
3. Pick one group large enough to measure but small enough to be safe. A single issuer's debit range is a typical first candidate.
4. In your gateway or orchestration layer, add a routing rule that sends a fixed share (for example 50%) of that BIN group to the second acquirer and keeps the rest on the primary as a holdout.
5. Run for long enough to cover a full billing cycle and payday effects, then compare approval rate, fraud rate, chargeback rate and all-in cost for the two arms.
6. If the result holds, move the full group; if not, roll back and record why.

**Prerequisites**

- A signed second acquirer with a live merchant ID in the right country, and pricing you understand line by line.
- Card credentials that work on both acquirers: network tokens or a vault you control, not tokens locked to the primary acquirer (see Issue 11).
- The same 3-D Secure and SCA set-up on both paths, the same merchant category code and a consistent descriptor.
- Reconciliation that can take two settlement files and two fee reports.
- A check of the primary acquirer contract for volume commitments or tiered pricing that the move could break.

**Metrics and expected impact**

- Primary: approval rate on the moved BIN group, test arm against holdout.
- Secondary: cost per approved transaction (basis points, interchange + scheme + markup), fraud and chargeback rates, and the share of first-try approvals.
- **Expected impact: [Assumption]**. It depends on the size of the gap in your own data, so it is sized in step 2 rather than taken from a benchmark. No public benchmark is used here, and no employer or client figures.

**Failure modes**

- **Lost volume tier at the primary acquirer.** The saving on one BIN group is wiped out by a price step-up on everything else. Early sign: the contract's volume bands. Fix: model it before you move.
- **Fraud moves with the traffic.** The new merchant ID starts with no history and may trip the acquirer's or the scheme's monitoring thresholds. Early sign: fraud-to-sales ratio on the new MID in week one. Fix: start small and ramp.
- **Token or credential mismatch.** Stored cards fail on the second path. Early sign: a spike in "invalid card" declines. Fix: test tokens end to end before launch.
- **Retry loops across acquirers.** A decline on one path triggers a retry on the other, which issuers may treat as abuse. Fix: one routing decision per attempt, logged.
- **Reconciliation breaks.** Two settlement cycles create timing gaps. Fix: reconcile daily for the first month.

**Owner.** Head of payments decides; payments engineering builds the rule; finance tracks cost per approval; risk watches fraud and chargebacks on the new MID; the partner manager handles both acquirer contracts.

**Cross-market column [CONFIRM sources before publishing]**

- **US:** large domestic issuer base and many debit networks; debit routing rules under the Federal Reserve's Regulation II apply. Check them before changing debit routing.
- **UK/EU:** interchange on consumer cards is capped in the EEA and the UK; the gain usually comes from approval rates and acquirer markup rather than interchange. SCA exemptions differ between acquirers, so compare exemption rates as well.
- **Africa:** acquirer choice is narrower, and local acquiring versus cross-border acquiring often matters more than BIN-level routing. Test local acquiring first [CONFIRM WITH AVI: South Africa experience].
- **APAC:** markets differ widely, with domestic schemes and local acquiring needs in several countries. Route by market before routing by BIN.

**What I'd do Monday**

1. Ask for 90 days of auth data by BIN and decline code (a direct data share with your processor is the cleanest way to get it).
2. Find the three BIN groups with the largest lost-approval value.
3. Read your primary acquirer contract for volume commitments.
4. Confirm your stored credentials work on a second acquirer.
5. Set up one 50/50 test with a holdout and a rollback rule.
